When you lean too much, you fall over. Here's what I suggest instead of “lean”, to help you avoid metaphorically falling over in your public speaking. 

Since my last post, a few of you have asked me, “What’s wrong with the word ‘lean’, anyway?” (In person, not in the comments. Thanks, folks, for helping boost my dreams of becoming a social media writing guru.)

As I have repeatedly discussed in my two decades as a writer-editor, jargon and buzzwords harm communication. This is true for both written and verbal communication. In fact, overusing buzzwords is the surest way to lose an audience and make even your best ideas sound idiotic.

Luckily for any executives out there looking to hire me as a speechwriter*, I can recommend much better ways to express yourself. 

- “Lean in”. You mean seize an opportunity, or exploit a resource for business advantage. But “lean” makes it sound like you don't know what those opportunities or resources are. State the specifics. Give context.
- “Lean forward”. You mean being proactive, taking bold action, or increasing appetite for risk. The phrase “lean forward” doesn't tell me what I'm supposed to do, other than maybe check if something is stuck to my shoe. Name the exact values and behaviours you wish to promote.
- “Lean toward”. You mean you have a mild-to-moderate preference for one option out of two or more. At least the metaphor works - unlike the last two - but I would still go for more straightforward phrases, like “I prefer” or “I like X more.”

And there you have it. Please spread this post far and wide. Help me build a better tomorrow where “lean” makes up less than 10% of the average meeting’s runtime. It's lean a pleasure.
